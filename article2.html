The Sovereignty Blueprint: A Smart Plan for Nepal's Future

By Yogesh Pokharel

October 2, 2026

Nepal is at a historic turning point. As our country prepares to move up from "Least Developed Country" status, the old ways of building infrastructure are no longer working. Right now, our national budget is under heavy strain. We are spending billions of rupees every winter to import expensive electricity, and we waste massive amounts of money every year just to fix broken roads. The old methods are simply too expensive to maintain.

"The Sovereignty Blueprint" offers a completely new plan. Instead of looking at our steep mountains \and deep valleys as problems, this blueprint treats Nepal’s unique geography as our greatest asset.

For decades, development in Nepal has made a major mistake: trying to flatten a vertical country. We see this when the government insists on building expensive, winding roads on fragile hills, or when we build power plants that only work well when the rivers run high in the summer. By shifting to a State-Led Synchronized Infrastructure Model, Nepal will finally stop fighting its own terrain and start using it wisely.

  2. Cut transport costs in the hills by 3 times: Using straight-line paths will make moving goods and people much cheaper.
  3. Protect our roads and farms in the Terai: Building smart drainage will stop floods from destroying infrastructure and crops. By working with our nature instead of against it—using gravity for transport, storing water for the winter, and managing heavy monsoon rains—this blueprint gives Nepal a clear roadmap to true economic independence.

Part 2: Moving High above the Hills | Choosing Ropeways Over Roads

For years, Nepal has followed a "road-first" policy. Unfortunately, this policy completely ignores the physical reality of our steep hills and mountains. To build a standard 16-foot-wide road on a steep slope, workers have to cut deep into the hillside and dump the soil down the slope. This destabilizes the soil and triggers heavy landslides every monsoon.

Aerial ropeway systems—ranging from simple gravity-powered cargo lines to advanced passenger cable cars—turn our vertical mountains into an advantage. Instead of wrapping a road around a mountain, a ropeway cuts straight across the air.

The difference between building a standard hilly feeder road and an aerial ropeway system is immense:

  1. 8 Times Faster to Build: It takes about 1 to 2 years to construct just 1 kilometer of a difficult mountain road. A ropeway can cover that same kilometer in just 3 to 4 months.
  2. 3 to 4 Times Cheaper: Building 1 kilometer of a hilly road costs between NRs. 50 Million and 100 Million. A ropeway system costs only NRs. 15 Million to 25 Million per kilometer.
  3. The Straight-Line Advantage: If two villages are separated by a deep river canyon, a car might need to travel 15 kilometers along winding hairpin turns to get from one side to the other. A ropeway can connect them with a 1-kilometer straight line through the air. This means ropeways are often 10 to 15 times cheaper per connection because they completely eliminate long, winding detours.
  4. Zero Land Damage: Roads destroy vast areas of valuable farms and forests. Ropeways require almost no land because they only touch the ground where the towers and stations stand.
  5. Immune to Disasters: Roads are regularly blocked or washed away by mudslides for 3 to 4 months during the rainy season. Ropeways operate safely high above the ground, making them completely immune to floods and landslides.Switching to ropeways also helps our economy using our own "White Gold" (Hydropower). Right now, buying foreign oil for trucks and cars drains our national savings. Because ropeways run on electricity, using them creates a steady daytime demand for our domestic hydropower. This keeps our money inside the country.

Furthermore, ropeways instantly change lives for rural farmers. For example, in remote hills, farmers cannot get fresh milk to the city before it spoils. They are forced to boil it down into khuwa using massive amounts of firewood, which cuts their profits by three times. Installing simple "Milkways"—like the famous one in Bhattedanda—allows fresh produce to reach markets in minutes, doubling household incomes within two years.

To make this happen, the government must stop measuring progress by how many kilometers of roads are dug. Instead, the National Planning Commission must measure "Connectivity-Heads"—how many people are successfully connected. By redirecting 15% of the local road budget toward a National Ropeway Grid in steep areas, Nepal can build a transport system that lasts.

Part 3: Storing Our Water | The Path to True Energy Independence

Nepal has a hidden problem with its electricity. The government often celebrates that we are a "Net Power Exporter," but this is a mathematical illusion. The reality is that our energy system is highly unstable and depends heavily on other countries.

Right now, Nepal can produce about 3,800 Megawatts of electricity during the rainy summer months. However, 92% of our power plants are "Run-of-River" systems. This means they do not store water; they simply generate electricity from whatever water happens to be flowing in the river at that exact moment.

  1. The Winter CrashIn the dry winter months, our rivers dry up, and our electricity production plummets from 3,800 Megawatts down to just 1,100 Megawatts.
  2. The DeficitOur country needs 2,400 Megawatts of power during winter peaks.
  3. The Foreign DependencyTo keep the lights on and prevent massive blackouts, Nepal is forced to import more than 1,000 Megawatts every day from India.

This winter shortage triggers a massive financial drain on our national treasury.

When societies inherit a version of history that presents peace as something generously granted by the powerful, the sacrifices of those who struggled for their own freedom can disappear from the story.

During the Summer Monsoon, we have an energy surplus and export power to India. However, because the market is flooded with power, we must sell our extra electricity at a very low price of around NRs. 7.11 per unit.

During the Winter Dry Season, our production drops, causing an energy deficit. Because we desperately need power to keep the grid alive, we are forced to buy it back from India at an expensive premium of NRs. 11.00 to NRs. 15.05 per unit.

In the first five months of the 2025/26 fiscal year, Nepal earned NRs. 18.26 Billion by exporting summer electricity. But because we lack water storage, a massive

chunk of that hard-earned money is immediately sent back across the border to buy winter electricity. We are essentially spending our national savings to keep the regional power grid stable, while our own domestic industries suffer from high energy costs.

To fix this, Nepal must build Reservoir Hydropower Projects—massive lakes that store water during the rainy summer so we can release it to generate electricity during the dry winter. This is too important to leave to private companies; the government must take charge. Other countries like Ethiopia and Norway achieved total energy independence by using strong, state-led models to build and control their own water reservoirs.

Nepal must immediately focus on building its own "National Batteries":

  1. Budhi Gandaki (1,200 Megawatts)This project alone will generate 1.41 Billion units of electricity during the dry winter. That is more than enough to completely wipe out our winter imports from India. Best of all, we do not need foreign loans to build it. The NRs. 5 per liter infrastructure tax that citizens pay on petroleum has already saved up over NRs. 170 Billion. This fund must be used to build Budhi Gandaki as a 100% state-owned project.
  2. Dudh Koshi(635)MegawattsThis is proven to be the most cost-effective storage project in Nepal. It must be built exclusively by the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) so that the profits stay within our borders. The Office of the Prime Minister should immediately freeze all new "Run-of-River" licenses for 3 years. Every single engineer and government resource must be redirected toward fast-tracking our major reservoir projects: Budhi Gandaki, Dudh Koshi, and Nalsingad.

    3. Part 4: The Alpine Frontier – Upgrading Our Tourism Economy

    Right now, Nepal’s tourism economy relies too heavily on just a few crowded destinations. Most visitors herd toward the Everest region, the Annapurna circuit, or Lumbini. This causes serious waste and environmental pollution in those specific areas, while leaving large parts of our country economically neglected. To reach our national goal of welcoming 2.5 million tourists every year, the government must lead the way in opening up our northern alpine assets—especially our untouched, high-altitude glaciers and hidden mountain vistas.

    Our current tourism model focuses mostly on high volume. It relies on Traditional Trekking, where visitors spend a modest $40 to $55 per day. This model requires heavy trail maintenance and sets up crowded teahouses, causing high environmental damage through poor waste management.

    Instead, the "Alpine Frontier" plan pivots directly toward Glacier and Alpine Luxury, focusing heavily on Value per Head. By building controlled, high-end eco-lodges and organizing targeted cable car access, Nepal can attract exclusive travelers who gladly spend $300 to $800 per day, resulting in a very low environmental footprint.

    The government must actively brand these hidden assets for the global market :

    1. Glacial TourismWhile glaciers in Europe are melting away rapidly, Nepal’s high-altitude glacial lakes—like Tsho Rolpa, Imja, and Kapuche—offer a unique "last chance to see" attraction for global travelers.
    2. Accessible VistasBy installing specialized tourist ropeways in breathtaking areas like the Ganesh Himal or Rolwaling ranges, we can provide immediate access to stunning scenery above 5,000 meters. This opens the majesty of the Himalayas to high-net-worth individuals, elderly travelers, and people with limited mobility who cannot go on a grueling 14-day trek. They can simply pay a premium for a quick, comfortable 20-minute aerial ride.

To put this new "Nepal Brand" on the global map, the state needs to act digitally and strategically. The government should create high-definition virtual tours and drone-mapped 3D views of our virgin peaks to present at international trade fairs. We should also offer a 100% tax waiver for international film crews that choose to shoot movies in these newly opened alpine zones. This is the exact, zero-cost method New Zealand used with The Lord of the Rings to turn unknown landscapes into global icons overnight.

Finally, the Office of the Prime Minister must pass the "Virgin Vista" Protection Act. This law will create a strict 10-year "No-Road Zone" for these designated alpine areas, mandating that access can only happen via foot paths or electric ropeways. This ensures we protect the high-value, pristine nature of the brand. The Nepal Tourism Board must also focus 70% of its promotional budget on the world's five highest-spending travel markets: the United States, Germany, Japan, the United Kingdom, and France.

In the flat terrain of the Terai plains, roads act as accidental dams. When heavy monsoon rains hit, the water has no natural way to escape. This causes a massive hidden crisis of "drowning roads" across our southern economic belt:

  1. Saturated SoilWater pools against the pavement and seeps directly beneath the blacktop. This weakens the soil foundation, causing "crocodile cracking" and massive potholes.
  2. The Maintenance ParadoxThe government spends billions of rupees every two years to lay down fresh asphalt. However, this money is completely wasted because it never fixes the root cause of the damage: the lack of a managed water exit.

    3. The blueprint solves this by introducing a mandatory design rule: for every kilometer of road built in the Terai, a lined drainage canal must be built at the same time on one side (the natural downhill or flow side).

    Conventional Terai roads rely on small, earthen side-drains that clog up easily with mud, leading to a pavement life of just 2 to 3 years before major repairs are needed. This leads to high, recurring annual patching costs and severe waterlogging in nearby farms.

The Integrated Canal-Road Model completely changes this equation. It features a deep, lined concrete canal designed for high-volume water discharge. This keeps the road dry and stable, extending the pavement life to 12 to 15 years and lowering maintenance to routine cleaning. Instead of flooding nearby crops, the canal safely carries runoff to the nearest natural stream, protecting surrounding farms.

This deep canal acts like a continuous suction point, drawing groundwater away from the road's foundation to keep the sub-base dry. During the dry season, this same canal serves a dual purpose: it can be used for minor farm irrigation or as a water source for fire hydrants in rural settlements.

For the state treasury, this design creates a 1:3 savings ratio. Adding a lined canal increases initial construction costs by about 20% to 25%. However, because the road does not need to be completely rebuilt every three years, the total cost over a 15-year lifecycle is 60% lower than our current, wasteful "Build-Destroy-Rebuild" cycle. This ensures that the Terai remains a reliable, 365-day functional trade spine that never vanishes during the monsoon.

To make this a reality, the Office of the Prime Minister must amend the Nepal Road Standard to make these large-scale one-sided drainage canals mandatory for all Terai roads wider than 7 meters. We must also allow the Department of Roads and the Department of Irrigation to pool their funding together for these hybrid projects. The goal is simple: by 2030, all National Pride Highways in the Terai must be bordered by integrated canals to eliminate monsoon road closures permanently.

By combining these four major projects, Nepal can move from being a "land-locked" state to a "logistics-optimized" state. This plan coordinates our unique geography with modern technology to create a strong, self-reliant economy:

  1. Vertical Growth (Ropeways & Alpine Tourism)We can use electric ropeways to conquer steep mountain terrain. This opens up our most valuable northern assets—like high-altitude glaciers—to premium global travelers. It successfully shifts our tourism model from low-spending budget travelers to high-yield luxury adventurers.
  2. The Energy Loop (Hydropower & Transport)Building state-owned water reservoirs provides clean, reliable electricity all year round. This domestic energy powers our national ropeway grid, completely stopping the outflow of billions of rupees for imported fossil fuels.
  3. The Terai Industrial Spine (Canal-Roads)While the mountains generate high-value tourism and sovereign energy, the stabilized canal-roads in the Terai ensure our national trade corridors stay safe and open. Industrial logistics can move smoothly 365 days a year, unaffected by heavy monsoon rains.

The True Cost of Inaction

If the government continues with its "Business as Usual" model over the next 10 years, the financial drain on Nepal will be severe

  1. In EnergyNepal will lose approximately NRs. 280 Billion purely to winter electricity import premiums. Under the Sovereignty Blueprint, we achieve zero winter imports and remain a profitable year-round power exporter.
  2. In LogisticsTrade logistics will continue to consume a painful 18% of our national GDP due to slow, winding roads. Moving to straight-line ropeways shrinks this cost to just 6% of GDP.
  3. In TourismRevenue will remain stagnant around a low $45 average daily spend per visitor. Upgrading to luxury alpine hubs pushes this past $300+ per day.
  4. In InfrastructureRepetitive, cyclical road repairs in the Terai will drain NRs. 350 Billion from the public treasury. The integrated canal standard brings a 70% reduction in monsoon-related pavement damage.

The government will map out 10 "Virgin Alpine Zones" and launch three pilot "Ropeway-to-Market" corridors in the hills alongside a 50 km canal-road pilot in the Terai.
