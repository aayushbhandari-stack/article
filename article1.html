HISTORY

EXPOSING THE COLONIAL BLUFF AND THE MYTH OF UNEARNED PEACE

By Yogesh Pokharel

October 2, 2026

Preface

This is not a history book.

History books are written by the comfortable, for the comfortable: footnoted, hedged, careful to give the executioner and the executed an equal hearing, as if there were two respectable sides to strapping a man to the mouth of a cannon. This is something else. This is the story of an empire that stole a continent and then spent a century writing the theft up as a favor, and of the men and women who refused to say thank you.

For two hundred years the British told a simple story about themselves: that they arrived among the primitive and the divided, and they left behind railways, courts and civilization, and that the violence of their arrival was a regrettable necessity, and the violence of their departure the fault of the ungrateful natives who couldn't wait a little longer for freedom to be handed down like a gift. It is one of the most successful lies in the history of the world and it survives for a simple reason: it was written by the winners, translated by the compliant and taught back to the descendants of the conquered as if it were their own memory.

It is not their memory. This is.

This book is not fair to the British Empire and it does not intend to be. Fairness is a courtesy extended between equals and there was nothing equal about a foundry in Lahore casting better artillery than Woolwich only to be starved out by bribery rather than vanquished in battle; nothing equal about a five-year-old king's hair being cut off in the name of civilization; nothing equal about ninety soldiers and two armored cars sealing the only exit of a walled garden and firing for ten minutes into a wedding-day crowd. You do not owe balance to a crime. You owe it the truth told with the full weight of what it actually was.

What follows is arranged not as a textbook but as a current; a single, rising argument that begins with the smoke of the Lahore foundries and ends with the guns of the Royal Indian Navy swiveled inland at Bombay because that is the shape the truth actually takes when you stop filing it by year and start reading it by cause and consequence. You will meet kings who were kidnapped as children and diamonds that were cut down into brooches. You will meet a teenager who taught an entire generation how to die without flinching and a woman who ran an underground army with an infant on her hip. You will meet the polite men in London who called a massacre a mistake and the man who spent twenty-one years crossing oceans to make sure it was understood as something else. And you will meet, again and again, the particular cruelty of an empire that preferred to buy a man's betrayal rather than earn his surrender because bought men are cheaper than dead ones, and because an empire built on bribery can always find another traitor when the last one grows a conscience.

Read it the way it was written: not as an argument to be weighed but as a debt to be acknowledged. The dead did not ask to be remembered fairly. They asked to be remembered.

MOVEMENT I — THE EMPIRE AND THE LION

Part I: The Myth of Modernity: Inside the Industrial Juggernaut of Lahore

For nearly half a century, the expanding, insatiable machine of the British East India Company ground its way across the Indian subcontinent, swallowing kingdoms through debt, deceit and devastating artillery fire. From the fertile plains of Bengal to the rugged hills of the Maratha confederacy, the redcoats of the Company brought a clinical, corporate tyranny. Yet, for all their imperial arrogance, their march halted abruptly at the banks of the Sutlej River.

Beyond that water lay a sovereign superpower that the British dared not provoke: the Sikh Empire of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. The standard imperial textbooks love to frame the European colonizer as the sole bringer of industrial and military modernity to Asia. It is a lie designed to retroactively justify theft.

The truth was forged in the blinding white heat of the royal foundries at Lahore. Under the sharp, visionary eye of the Maharaja, Punjab did not merely import weapons; it mastered and perfected the science of metallurgy. Walk into the foundries of the Lahore Arsenal in the 1820s and 1830s, and you would find not backward artisans, but a highly sophisticated, industrialized juggernaut.

Punjabi smiths, working alongside European mercenaries who had fled the Napoleonic Wars, were casting brass, bronze and iron ordnance that stunned British observers.

The Heavy Steel of the Khalsa: Outgunning the Royal Arsenal

These were not crude cannons. They were masterworks of engineering: heavy, long-range siege guns and highly mobile horse artillery pieces adorned with intricate Persian inscriptions and the imagery of the Gurus, yet built with a precision that matched or exceeded the royal foundries of Woolwich in London. The Khalsa artillery possessed standard calibers, superior carriages and a devastating explosive yield.

When British political agents like Alexander Burnes visited Lahore, they looked at the massive rows of perfectly aligned cannons and realized a terrifying truth: in a straight, conventional artillery duel, the East India Company would be utterly annihilated. This material reality created a profound psychological deadlock. The British, accustomed to treating native rulers with aloof contempt, were forced to play the role of humble, equal partners.

They signed the Treaty of Perpetual Friendship in 1809 because they had no alternative. They sent lavish gifts, including heavy English cart horses that Ranjit Singh famously mocked as too slow. All the while, imperial agents covertly gauged the strength of the state, hiding their panic behind grand darbars and diplomatic smiles.

The Coward's Waiting Game: Treason Over Valor

The British did not possess the stomach to face the Fauj-i-Khas, the elite, French-trained brigade of the Khalsa Army, on an open field where Punjabi steel and tactical brilliance would dictate the terms. The Akalis, the fanatical, blue-turbaned Sikh warriors, operated as a psychological terror weapon, their fearless charges legendary for breaking the morale of any force in their path. The British knew that a war with Punjab while Ranjit Singh was at the height of his powers would not be a colonial expedition; it would be a war of survival that could break the Company's back and drive them into the sea.

So, the "brave" empire played a coward’s waiting game. They turned diplomacy into an art of vultures. For decades, British spies and residents stationed along the border watched the health of the aging Maharaja with meticulous, sickening anticipation.

They mapped the internal rivalries of the Lahore court, identified the fault lines between the Sikh aristocracy and the Dogra prime ministers and waited for the heavy hammers of the Lahore foundries to fall silent. They knew the empire’s fatal flaw: it was held together by the sheer, singular willpower of one man. When the Lion of Punjab faltered, suffered strokes and finally closed his eyes in 1839, the British didn't march in with flags flying; they slipped through the fractures they had spent thirty years buying and engineering.

The invasion that followed years later was not a triumph of British military valor; it was a festival of bribery, political assassination and the treason of bought commanders who broke the back of the finest army Asia had ever seen, tearing it apart from the inside out.

Part II: The Engineered Fall of a Kingdom

The Vacuum After the Lion

The smoke from Maharaja Ranjit Singh’s funeral pyre had barely cleared before the vultures in Calcutta and London began to tighten their grip. For forty years, the British Empire had cowered behind the Sutlej River, paralyzed by the sheer material and martial weight of a unified Punjab. But with the Lion of Punjab gone, the British unleashed their true weapon of conquest. It was not the courage of their infantry, but a systematic campaign of political sabotage, espionage and the calculated buying of men’s souls.

The fall of the Sikh Empire was not a defeat born on the battlefield; it was an execution engineered in the shadows of the Lahore Darbar, orchestrated by treacherous prime ministers and commanders who sold their people for British gold and titles.

The Engineered Vacuum and the Blood in the Palace

The British political residency at Ludhiana transformed overnight into a nerve center of subversion. British agents well understood that the structural vulnerability of the Khalsa Empire lay in its centralization: it required a singular, towering authority to hold the fiercely independent factions of the nobility and the army together. With the ascension of Ranjit Singh's eldest son, Kharak Singh, and his brilliant grandson, Nau Nihal Singh, the British faced a dangerous scenario: a continuation of capable, sovereign rule.

To break this lineage, British machinations exploited the fatal greed of the Dogra brothers: Gulab Singh, Dhian Singh and Suchet Singh. These brothers, who had risen to immense wealth and power under Ranjit Singh’s meritocratic rule, harbored ambitions that transcended mere courtly titles; they wanted sovereign kingdoms of their own. Dhian Singh Dogra, serving as Prime Minister, initiated a toxic whisper campaign, falsely convincing the royal family and the Khalsa army of treasons that did not exist, leading to a bloody chain of internal assassinations.

Within a few chaotic years, the palace at Lahore ran red with royal blood. Kharak Singh was slowly poisoned to death. On the very day of his funeral, a mysteriously collapsing stone archway killed his brilliant heir, Nau Nihal Singh, an event widely suspected of being orchestrated by Dhian Singh.

Soon after, Maharaja Sher Singh was brutally gunned down by the Sandhawalia sardars. By 1843, the only remaining heir to the throne of the mighty Lion of Punjab was a five-year-old child, Duleep Singh, shielded by his mother, Maharani Jind Kaur.

The Rise of the Khalsa Panchayats and British Panic

As the aristocracy tore itself apart in a frenzy of greed, the true keepers of Ranjit Singh's legacy rose from the ranks of the common soldiers. The Fauj-i-Khas and the wider Khalsa Army looked at the treasonous nobility with profound disgust. Reviving ancient traditions, the soldiers formed Panchayats: democratic military councils elected from the ranks.

These councils overthrew the authority of the corrupt, aristocratic officers and took direct control of the army's movements. This democratic militarism struck absolute terror into the hearts of the British. The Governor General of India, Lord Hardinge, and the Commander-in-Chief, Sir Hugh Gough, realized they were no longer dealing with a standard feudal state that could be managed by bribing a king.

They were dealing with an armed, highly politicized, ideologically driven citizen-army that possessed the finest artillery in Asia. The British papers framed the Khalsa Army as a "mutinous, lawless rabble" to justify aggression, but their private letters revealed an agonizing dread. They knew that if this peasant-led army marched across the Sutlej, it would spark a revolution that could ignite the rest of occupied India.

The British could not win a clean war against the Khalsa Panchayats. They needed the ultimate betrayal. They needed the commanders of the Sikh Army to actively fight for the enemy.

The Grand Treason: Lal Singh and Tej Singh

When the First Anglo-Sikh War finally erupted in late 1845, the Khalsa Army marched south to defend their homeland with fierce, unyielding devotion. They crossed the Sutlej not as invaders, but as protectors of a dying empire. Yet, standing at their head were two of the most monstrous traitors in global military history: Raja Lal Singh, the Prime Minister, and Misr Tej Singh, the Commander-in-Chief.

Both men had already entered into secret pacts with British political agents like Major George Broadfoot, trading the blood of their soldiers for guaranteed security of their personal wealth and future estates. The raw battlefield reality of this treachery was agonizingly laid bare at Mudki and Ferozeshah. At the Battle of Ferozeshah (December 1845), the Khalsa soldiers fought with a ferocity that shattered the British lines.

The heavy Lahore artillery tore the British regiments to pieces. European mercenaries and veteran British officers fell by the hundreds. By the night of December 21, the British army was in utter disarray, out of ammunition, starving and frozen.

Lord Hardinge himself admitted that the empire was lost; he ordered his state papers to be burned and his royal sword delivered to his son, expecting total annihilation the following morning. The British Empire in Asia hung by a single thread. The next morning, the fresh, uncommitted reserve forces of the Khalsa Army under Tej Singh arrived on the battlefield.

A single, decisive charge would have obliterated the remaining British forces and driven the East India Company out of northern India permanently. Instead, while looking upon a panicked, defenseless enemy, Tej Singh deliberately committed an act of supreme cowardice: he ordered his victorious army to turn around and retreat. The soldiers argued, cursed their commander and wept with rage, but the chain of command was shattered.

The Slaughter at Sobraon: Stolen in the Dark

At the Battle of Sobraon (February 1846), the betrayal reached its sickening climax. The Khalsa soldiers were entrenched with their backs to the Sutlej River. As the British launched a desperate assault, Lal Singh and Tej Singh fled the field early.

Before leaving, Tej Singh ordered the destruction of the pontoon bridge spanning the swollen river—effectively trapping his own army within a killing zone. The Khalsa soldiers did not surrender. Surrounded, outnumbered and utterly betrayed by their own leaders, they fought to the absolute last man.

They advanced into British bayonets with swords drawn, while the British horse artillery moved to the riverbanks and systematically bombarded thousands of drowning Punjabi soldiers attempting to swim across. The British did not conquer Punjab through superior strategy, moral fiber, or martial bravery. They crawled into Lahore through a bridge built from the treason of Lal Singh and the Dogra brothers, who were rewarded for their treachery by being handed the kingdom of Jammu and Kashmir under the Treaty of Amritsar.

The standard imperial historical narrative praises this as a great British victory, but the mud of Sobraon holds the true story—an empire stolen in the dark from an army that could never be beaten face-to-face.

Part III: The Psychological Cage and the Fracture of Punjabiyat

The Post-Annexation Crisis of Control

With the young Maharaja Duleep Singh safely exiled and isolated in the fog of England—stripped of his hair, his faith, and his crown—the British Board of Administration turned its clinical focus from the royal palace to the wider population. Stripping a child king of his identity was a surgical victory, but the British still faced a massive, ticking time bomb: they had swallowed a vast territory inhabited by an unbending, deeply politicized and highly trained populace. The psychological shockwaves of the Anglo-Sikh wars still lingered in every village, and the colonizers knew that if the peasantry, the demobilized soldiers and the urban workers ever found common ground, the imperial occupation would be swept into the Sutlej within a matter of weeks.

To ensure that the heavy hammers of the Lahore foundries never struck iron again, the colonial administrators, led by the Lawrence brothers, John and Henry, unleashed a calculated, sociological assault designed to fracture the Punjabi psyche, disarm the populace, and permanently segregate the collective public mind.

The Physical Castration: The Great Disarmament of 1849

The very first act of the new colonial administration was to strip Punjab of its physical capacity to wage war. Under the General Disarmament Proclamation, the possession of any weapon—from the massive siege guns of the Khalsa to the traditional kirpan (sacred sword) carried by every initiated Sikh—was declared a capital crime. British columns marched across the Majha and Doaba regions like locusts.

They did not just seize weapons from armorers; they searched private homes, tearing up floorboards and digging through granaries. Within a year, over 119,000 weapons—swords, matchlocks, spears, and shields—were confiscated, melted down, or dumped into rivers. The proud martial heritage of a sovereign populace was systematically converted into raw scrap metal.

Simultaneously, the economic backbone of the old empire was dismantled. The magnificent royal foundries and arsenals of Lahore were physically demolished. The master smiths, engineers and metallurgists who had once out-manufactured the royal arsenals of Europe were forced into menial agrarian labor or driven into poverty.

By erasing the physical tools and the industrial knowledge of weaponry, the British sought to instill a deep, permanent sense of helplessness—a psychological realization that resistance was no longer materially possible.

The "Martial Races" Myth: Segregation by Classification

With the physical weapons gone, the British turned to a far more insidious tool of control: pseudo-scientific sociology. They realized that blunt, indiscriminate brutality would eventually unite the diverse population of Muslims, Sikhs and Hindus against them.

To prevent this unity, they began to categorize, classify and isolate the Punjabi public using the toxic "Martial Races" framework. Invented by colonial administrators and military strategists, this theory claimed that only certain, highly specific communities possessed the biological and hereditary traits required for warfare. The British meticulously mapped the caste, tribal, and religious identities of Punjab, creating rigid hierarchies where none had existed in such a polarized form.

  • The "Martial" Select: Certain land-owning Jat Sikhs, Punjabi Muslims and Dogras were highly praised, elevated and recruited into the British Indian Army.
  • The "Non-Martial" Majority: The urban mercantile classes, lower castes and intellectual communities were branded as inherently cowardly, weak and unfit for military service.

    • This was not a harmless system of military recruitment; it was a psychological weapon of mass segregation. By giving preferred land grants, water rights through newly engineered canal colonies, and prestigious military employment only to specific sub-caste groups, the British manufactured an artificial aristocracy.
