HISTORY

THE PRICE OF BLOOD: EXPOSING THE COLONIAL BLUFF AND THE MYTH OF UNEARNED PEACE

By Yogesh Pokharel

October 2, 2026

Preface

This is not a history book.

History books are written by the comfortable, for the comfortable: footnoted, hedged, careful to give the executioner and the executed an equal hearing, as if there were two respectable sides to strapping a man to the mouth of a cannon. This is something else. This is the story of an empire that stole a continent and then spent a century writing the theft up as a favor, and of the men and women who refused to say thank you.

For two hundred years the British told a simple story about themselves: that they arrived among the primitive and the divided, and they left behind railways, courts and civilization, and that the violence of their arrival was a regrettable necessity, and the violence of their departure the fault of the ungrateful natives who couldn't wait a little longer for freedom to be handed down like a gift. It is one of the most successful lies in the history of the world and it survives for a simple reason: it was written by the winners, translated by the compliant and taught back to the descendants of the conquered as if it were their own memory.

This book is not fair to the British Empire and it does not intend to be. Fairness is a courtesy extended between equals and there was nothing equal about a foundry in Lahore casting better artillery than Woolwich only to be starved out by bribery rather than vanquished in battle; nothing equal about a five-year-old king's hair being cut off in the name of civilization; nothing equal about ninety soldiers and two armored cars sealing the only exit of a walled garden and firing for ten minutes into a wedding-day crowd. You do not owe balance to a crime. You owe it the truth told with the full weight of what it actually was.

What follows is arranged not as a textbook but as a current; a single, rising argument that begins with the smoke of the Lahore foundries and ends with the guns of the Royal Indian Navy swiveled inland at Bombay because that is the shape the truth actually takes when you stop filing it by year and start reading it by cause and consequence. You will meet kings who were kidnapped as children and diamonds that were cut down into brooches. You will meet a teenager who taught an entire generation how to die without flinching and a woman who ran an underground army with an infant on her hip. You will meet the polite men in London who called a massacre a mistake and the man who spent twenty-one years crossing oceans to make sure it was understood as something else. And you will meet, again and again, the particular cruelty of an empire that preferred to buy a man's betrayal rather than earn his surrender because bought men are cheaper than dead ones, and because an empire built on bribery can always find another traitor when the last one grows a conscience.

Read it the way it was written: not as an argument to be weighed but as a debt to be acknowledged. The dead did not ask to be remembered fairly. They asked to be remembered.

MOVEMENT I — THE EMPIRE AND THE LION

Part I: The Myth of Modernity: Inside the Industrial Juggernaut of Lahore

Colonial powers frequently justified expansion by presenting themselves as agents of civilization. Infrastructure, administration and education were sometimes used as evidence that colonial rule had produced progress.

Such claims, however, cannot be understood without examining the broader political and economic systems in which those developments occurred.

Roads, railways, ports and administrative systems can have lasting value. But their existence alone does not answer the historical question of why they were built, who controlled them and whose interests they primarily served.

Who Pays the Price?

Behind every major political transformation are ordinary people. Soldiers, workers, farmers, merchants and families experience the consequences of decisions made by governments and institutions far more powerful than themselves.

The human cost of conflict cannot be reduced to statistics. Every number represents an individual life, a family and a community affected by events beyond their control.

History and the Comfortable Narrative

Historical narratives are rarely completely neutral. What gets recorded, what gets remembered and what gets forgotten are all influenced by the institutions that preserve historical records.

This does not mean that every historical account is deliberately false. It means that readers should examine historical claims carefully and consider the perspectives that may have been left outside the dominant narrative.

The executioner and the executed do not necessarily stand on equal ground simply because both can be mentioned in the same historical account.

The Myth of Unearned Peace

Peace deserves to be understood not merely as the absence of open conflict, but as a condition shaped by political power, economic relationships and historical memory.

When societies inherit a version of history that presents peace as something generously granted by the powerful, the sacrifices of those who struggled for their own freedom can disappear from the story.

A more complete understanding requires asking difficult questions: Who paid the price? Who gained from the arrangement? Whose voices were recorded? And whose experiences were forgotten?

Remembering the Cost

History should not simply be a collection of dates, treaties and names. It should also be an examination of human choices and their consequences.

Understanding the price behind historical events allows us to look beyond simplified narratives and examine the forces that shaped the world we inherited.

Peace may be valuable, but understanding how that peace was created is equally important. The past cannot be changed, but the way we understand it can determine how we approach the future.
